AbstractClostridioides difÞcileis one of the most common nosocomial gastrointestinal
pathogens, and recurrence is a problematic issue because approximately 20e30% of patients
experience at least one episode of recurrence, even after treatment with a therapeutic drug
of choice forC. difÞcileinfection (CDI), such as vancomycin. CDI recurrence has a multifacto-
rial complex mechanism, in which gut microbiota disruption coincident with viableC. difÞcile
spores, is considered the most important factor. The effectiveness of an anti-C. difÞcileanti-
microbial agent against CDI cannot guarantee its inhibitory effect onC. difÞcilespores and
vice versa. However, an antimicrobial agent, such as Þdaxomicin, which has a good inhibitory
effect on bothC. difÞcilevegetative cells and spores is assumed to not only treat CDI but also
prevent its recurrence. Prolonged adherence to the exosporium has been proposed as a
possible mechanism of inhibiting spores, and as a result, redesigning anti-C. difÞcileantimicro-
bial agents with the ability to adhere to the exosporium may provide another pathway for the
development of anti-C. difÞcilespore agents. For example, vancomycin lacks an inhibitory ef-
fect againstC. difÞcilespores, but a vancomycin-loaded spore-targeting iron oxide nanopar-
ticle that selectively binds toC. difÞcilespores has been developed to successfully delay
spore germination. Some new antimicrobial agents in phase II clinical trials, including cadazo-
lid and ridinilazole, have shown exceptional anti-C. difÞcileand spore-inhibiting effects that
can be expected to not only treat CDI but also prevent its recurrence in the future.

Introduction
Clostridioides difﬁcileis one of the most common nosoco-
mial gastrointestinal pathogens, with clinical presentations
ranging from mild diarrhea and pseudomembranous colitis
to toxic megacolon, with a mortality of up to 25e40%.
1e9
Severe colitis develops in approximately 3% of patients
withC. difﬁcileinfection (CDI) who may have paradoxical
decrease in the amount of diarrhea after ileus or toxic
megacolon developed.
3,10
Diagnosis of CDI relies on the
detection of toxigenicC. difﬁcile, its toxins or toxin genes
in stool.
9,11
Metronidazole was suggested for mild CDI and vanco-
mycin was recommended for severe CDI.
8
Nevertheless,
oral vancomycin and ﬁdaxomicin have been suggested to
replace metronidazole as the therapeutic choice for either
mild-moderate or severe CDI in the Clinical Practice
Guidelines forC. difﬁcileInfection in Adults and Children:
2017 Update by the Infectious Diseases Society of America
(IDSA) and Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America
(SHEA).
12
For patients with severe and fulminant forms of
CDI presenting as ileus, oral intake of vancomycin or
ﬁdaxomicin is disrupted because of the obstruction of the
gut because ﬁdaxomicin can only be administered orally;
and vancomycin, either administered orally or intra-
rectally. This leads to marked disruption of the microbiota,
leading to prolonged loss of colonization resistance toC.
difﬁcileand dense colonization by vancomycin-resistant
Enterococcus,K. pneumoniae, andE. coli.
13
Although antimicrobial resistance is not clinically prob-
lematic, treating CDI with metronidazole and vancomycin is
associated with a high treatment failure rate and high
relapse rate.
14
According to the Clinical Practice Guidelines
by IDSA/SHEA, oral vancomycin was still the drug of choice
for recurrentC. difﬁcileassociated diarrhea, but the rate
of second recurrence rate is as high as 22.6e41.8%, despite
successful treatment with vancomycin.
15
The high recur-
rence rate of CDI after treatment might be due to the
generation of spores that lead to persistence of CDI.
16,17
The biology of spores in the pathogenesis ofC.
difﬁcile
Owing to its strict anaerobic requirements, the infectious
and transmissible morphotype ofC. difﬁcileis a dormant
spore, which is characterized by resistance to heat, oxy-
gen, and common disinfectants, such as ethanol-based
hand sanitizers.
16,18,19
The sporeeresistance properties
are attributed to a low water content (25e60% of wet
weight), elevated levels of dipicolonic acid (25% of core dry
weight) chelated with calcium, and the saturation of DNA
witha/b-type small acid soluble proteins.
16,20
The spores ofC. difﬁcilehas the innermost spore
compartment known as the spore core, which contains the
spore DNA, RNA and most enzymes.
16
The spore core is
encased in three protective layers: the ﬁrst is the com-
pressed inner membrane protein surrounded by the germ
cell wall, which has a very low permeability to small mol-
ecules, including water, and protects the core from DNA-
damaging molecules, the second is the peptidoglycan cor-
tex, a coat that predominantly consists of proteins, and the
third is the exosporium (the outermost layer), which pre-
dominantly consists of glycoproteins.
16
During CDI,C. difﬁcileinitiates a sporulation pathway
that produces dormant spores that lead to persistence and
dissemination of CDI within hospitalized patients, and the
pathway is triggered by environmental stimuli such as
nutrient starvation, quorum sensing and other unidentiﬁed
stress factors.
16,17
WhenC. difﬁcilespores are ingested,
the spores are capable of enduring the acidic conditions of
the stomach and then transition to a metabolically active
state (germinate) in the small intestine.
21
Germination of
C. difﬁcilespores and the growth of vegetative forms take
place only in the lower gastrointestinal tract where the
oxygen concentration is very low.
22,23
Some contents of the
intestine, such as bile acids, induce germination of the
spores into an actively replicating vegetative cell.
23,24
Taurocholate, a conjugated primary bile acid in the small
intestine, has the ability to promote germination and to
inﬂuence vegetative growth ofC. difﬁcile; in contrast,
chenodeoxycholate, another primary bile acid, inhibits
spore germination inC. difﬁcileand acts both as a
competitive inhibitor of taurocholate and a suppressor of
vegetative growth.
24,25
The efﬁciency of germination varies
between clinical strains ofC. difﬁcileand whether it is
associated with ribotype, disease severity or therapeutic
failure needs further investigation.
23,26
Rationale of the implication of spore inhibition
for the prevention of recurrence ofC. difﬁcile
infection: is it the absolute and only solution?
Recurrence is a problematic issue in CDI, with 20e30% of
patients facing at least one episode of recurrence even
when treated with a therapeutic drug of choice for CDI.
21,27
In a simulated CDI gut model, although vancomycin suc-
cessfully treated the initial episode of CDI, recurrence
occurred approximately 18 days after therapy cessation, in
which the germination of spores ofC. difﬁcilein the envi-
ronment of a bioﬁlm was noted.
28
The recurrence rates of
clinical patients with CDI receiving metronidazole and
vancomycin therapy, two of the most commonly used anti-
C. difﬁcileantibiotics, were 27.1% and 24.0%, respec-
tively.
27
The recurrence could be due to either reinfection

27
CDI recurrence has a multi-factorial complex mecha-
nism, but gut microbiota disruption coincident with viable
C. difÞcilespores is considered as the most important
factor.
28,29
Of course, targeting spores ofC. difÞcileis not
the only way to resolve the troublesome CDI recurrence
problem; multiple factors, including gut microbiota status
and the ribotype of theC. difÞcile(with different spore
germination efÞciency) also inßuences the recurrence rate
of CDI.
26
For example, rifaximin, though lacking spore-
inhibiting effects, can prevent the recurrence of CDI
when given immediately after completing standard anti-CDI
antibiotics through its relative sparing of enteric micro-
biota.
30e32
The ribotype ofC. difÞcilewith different spore
germination efÞciencies will inßuence the recurrent rate of
CDI; for example, ribotype 027 isolates exhibit signiÞcantly
higher germination efÞciencies in the presence of sodium
taurocholate than ribotype 106 and ribotype 078.
26
Even with the inßuence of the gut microbiota condition
and the ribotype of CDI, developing antimicrobial agents
with both anti-C. difÞcileand spore-inhibiting effects
have been proven to provide a way to not only treat CDI
but also prevent its recurrence, including Þdaxomicin, one
of the drugs of choice for CDI, and cadazolid and rid-
inilazole, two antimicrobial agents still in phase II clinical
trials (Table 1).
33e37
The application of the spore-inhibiting effect
of current anti-C. difÞcileantimicrobial agents
in the therapy ofC. difÞcileinfection and its
recurrence
The effectiveness of an anti-C. difÞcileantimicrobial agent
toward CDI cannot guarantee its inhibitory effect on spores
ofC. difÞcileand vice versa.
38e40
Though many antimi-
crobial agents are regarded as drugs of choice for CDI, such
as Þdaxomicin and vancomycin, their inhibitory effects on
C. difÞcilespores are diverse, which might be due to their
different adherent and inhibitory effects toward spores of
C. difÞcile
38,39
; in contrast, tigecycline and piperacillin/
tazobactam, two commonly used antimicrobial agents in
the clinic, both have a dose-dependent inhibitory effect on
spore production at subminimum inhibitory concentrations
(sub-MICs) in-vitro, but their clinical application is limited
because of their inadequate therapeutic effect on
CDI.
16,40,41
If an antimicrobial agent, such as Þdaxomicin,
has both anti-vegetative cell and anti-spore effects toward
C. difÞcile, it is assumed to be able to not only achieve a
therapeutic effect but also lower the recurrence rate.
33
In
contrast, if an antimicrobial agent, such as vancomycin, has
only anti-vegetative cell effects but lacks anti-spore effects
towardC. difÞcile, it will be hypothetically only achieving a
therapeutic effect but with a possible high recurrence rate
after therapy unless there is some modiÞcation of the
antimicrobial agents to strengthen its effect on spores.
38,39
Fidaxomicin, a drug used for CDI therapy, can inhibit both
the sporulation and the outgrowth of a vegetative cell from
the germinating spore, but its effect on the germination of
C. difÞcilespores is limited (Table 1).
30,42e45
Fidaxomicin
has a persistent inhibitory effect on sporulation when addedto early-stationary-phase cells inC. difÞcilestrains,
including the epidemic NAP1/BI/027 strain at sub-MICs, and
thus prevents subsequent growth and toxin production in-
vitro; in contrast, vancomycin and metronidazole do not
inhibit sporulation at similar sub-MICs.
30,43,44
In contrast to
its inhibitory effect on sporulation, Þdaxomicin was found to
have no inhibitory effect on the initiation ofC. difÞcile
spore germination by analyzing the kinetics of the initiation
of spore germination.
42
The spore-inhibiting effect of Þdaxomicin has been
illustrated in clinical trials, although the clinical cure rates
with Þdaxomicin were similar to those with vancomycin in
both the modiÞed intention-to-treat analysis (88.2% with
Þdaxomicin and 85.8% with vancomycin) and per-protocol
analysis (92.1% and 89.8%, respectively), signiÞcantly fewer
patients in the Þdaxomicin group than in the vancomycin
group had a recurrence of the infection, in both the modi-
Þed intention-to-treat analysis (15.4%vs.25.3%,PZ0.005)
and per-protocol analysis (13.3%vs.24.0%,PZ0.004).
46
The lower recurrence rate following Þdaxomicin therapy
compared to vancomycin was also due to its lower effect on
the microbiota.
15,47e50
In the mice model, Þdaxomicin
reduced the proportion of Clostridial growth to a lesser
extent, but increased that of Bacteroidia, resulting in less
disturbance in microbiota diversity.
47
The lesser inßuence
on the microbiota composition of Þdaxomicin, compared to
vancomycin, promoted faster microbial recovery in the gut,
and had more colonization resistance toC. difÞcile.
48e50
Prolonged adherence to the exosporium has been pro-
posed as a possible way of inhibiting spores; as a result,
redesigning anti-C. difÞcileantimicrobial agents with the
ability to adhere to the exosporium may provide another
pathway for the development of anti-C. difÞcilespore
agents.
38,39
Vancomycin, although considered as one of the
drugs of choice for CDI, was found to have no inhibitory
effect on the initiation ofC. difÞcilespore germination by
analyzing the kinetics of the initiation of spore germina-
tion.
42
Thus, the recurrence rate after vancomycin therapy
was as high as 24.0%.
27
although vancomycin itself has no
effect onC. difÞcilespores, a vancomycin-loaded spore-
targeting iron oxide nanoparticle (van-IONP) that selec-
tively binds toC. difÞcilespores has been developed to
successfully delay spore germination.
38

Conclusion
Developing an antimicrobial agent with a good inhibitory
effect on bothC. difÞcilevegetative cells and spores can
be expected to not only treat CDI but also prevent its
recurrence, such as Þdaxomicin. Cadazolid and ridinilazole
possess a spore-inhibiting effect in-vitro, and thus decrease
the recurrence of CDI after cessation of the drug in phase II
and III clinical trials. Some small molecules and compounds
were noted to have spore-inhibiting effects in-vitro, but the
determination of their ÒrealÓ clinical efÞcacy requires
further clinical trials.
