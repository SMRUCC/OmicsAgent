## IntroductionNutrients are key drivers in shaping the ecology of organisms within an environment [1]. In the mammalian gastrointestinal tract, nutrient availability is one factor controlling the diversity of the commensal bacteria, which in turn influences host immune responses, metabolism, and other critical processes [2,3]. Transition metals that include iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), manganese (Mn), and nickel (Ni) are trace elements necessary for all bacteria by serving as structural components in proteins and as enzymatic cofactors [4]. Limited metal availability initiates stress responses that reprogram metabolic pathways, increase production of proteins involved in metal uptake, and alter virulence factor expression [5]. Yet, maintaining intracellular metal pools must be balanced with avoiding toxic high metal concentrations. The coordinated control of metal availability is referred to as metallostasis [5].

*Clostridioides difficile* is a spore-forming bacterium that typically colonizes the intestines following dysbiosis, or disruption of the commensal microbiota [6]. Spores entering the intestines subsequent to dysbiosis germinate into vegetative *C. difficile*, which produce toxins that trigger a robust inflammatory response that further destabilizes the resident microbiota [7]. Throughout the cycle of *C. difficile* colonization, infection, and transmission, metal availability is controlled through the actions of the microbiota that may compete for those metals and by the immune response that limits metal concentrations in a process termed nutritional immunity. Here, we provide a brief overview of recent advancements in understanding the factors that control metal availability during *C. difficile* infection (CDI) and how *C. difficile* responds to those shifting metal concentrations to successfully colonize the intestines.

## Transition metal availability and the microbiotaApproximately90% of the gut bacteria in adult humans are from the phyla Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, with Actinobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, and Proteobacteria collectively making up less than10% of the remaining microbiota [8]. This diverse microbiota protects against overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria through a process called colonization resistance [9]. Disruption of colonization resistance is a primary risk factor that increases susceptibility to CDI and can be caused by antibiotic use or underlying inflammatory disorders [10,11]. Large fluctuations in nutrient metal availability, either through supplementation or deficiency, can similarly alter the microbiota composition to decrease colonization resistance (Figure1) [12,13].

Nearly all bacteria require Fe, thus changes in dietary Fe availability can impact the abundances of bacteria in the intestines [13,14]. Limited Fe can force competition, allowing bacteria such as members of the Enterobacteriaceae to outcompete other bacteria through multiple mechanisms including the production of high Fe-affinity siderophores that capture ferric iron (Fe^3+) [12,15,16]. Consistent with this notion, an increase in the proportion of *Escherichia coli* was observed in mice fed low Fe diets and was associated with increased transcription of siderophore import genes [17]. Alternatively, Fe supplementation in conjunction with inflammation also leads to changes in the microbiota characterized by an increase in Proteobacteria [18] and a decrease in beneficial butyrate-producing Clostridia including *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* and *Ruminococcus bromii* [19]. While there is variation in the specific populations affected by Fe deficiency and supplementation, multiple studies support Enterobacteriaceae blooms as a biomarker of microbiota destabilization [20]. Indeed, in a mouse CDI model, Enterobacteriaceae blooms following low-dose antibiotic treatments predicts susceptibility to *C. difficile* colonization [21]. In humans, adults symptomatically and asymptomatically colonized with *C. difficile* have higher abundances of Enterobacteriaceae compared to non-colonized individuals [22]. A similar trend is observed in elderly patients with CDI, where *C. difficile* is associated with increased *E. coli* and decreased *F. prausnitzii* abundances [23]. Despite similar patterns of dysbiosis between microbiota experiencing changes in Fe availability and microbiota during CDI, there is limited evidence that directly links dietary Fe to CDI susceptibility. A possible explanation could be that changes in Fe availability only affect CDI susceptibility in conjunction with other CDI risk factors. In accordance with this idea, supplementation of another dietary metal, Zn, only allowed *C. difficile* to colonize when combined with antibiotics. In this study, excess dietary Zn increased the proportion of *Enterococcus* and Clostridia XI while decreasing *Turicibacter* populations [24]. The excess Zn-induced dysbiosis then lowered the dose of antibiotics necessary to reduce colonization resistance against *C. difficile* [24,25]. Overall, as necessary nutrients, extreme changes in Fe and Zn concentrations can drastically influence the microbiota and synergize with other risk factors to alter susceptibility to CDI.

## Inflammation and nutritional immunity: a changing landscapeDuring CDI, toxin-mediated intestinal inflammation induces host immune responses that change metal availability (Figure2). *C. difficile* produces two primary toxins, TcdA and TcdB, involved in intestinal inflammation and epithelial damage [26]. The host immune response to these toxins is in part characterized by an influx of neutrophils that release cytosolic calprotectin (CP) [7]. CP is a heterodimeric protein of S100A8 and S100A9 that sequesters divalent first row transition metals at two sites at the dimer interface [27] to limit bioavailable Zn, Fe, Ni, Mn, and copper (Cu) [28,29–32]. Neutrophils and epithelial cells also release lipocalin-2, or neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), during inflammation [33]. Lipocalin-2 interferes with bacterial Fe uptake by binding to catecholate siderophores [33]. Fecal CP and lipocalin-2 concentrations are clinically used as biomarkers of inflammation severity [34,35] and shape the gut environment by altering metal availability.

While CP and lipocalin-2 limit metal acquisition, toxin-mediated epithelial damage and edema cause intestinal hemorrhaging and the release of Fe-containing heme through hemoglobin degradation [36–38]. Excessive heme accumulation is toxic to *C. difficile* [36], requiring the bacterium to adapt to high levels of luminal heme for survival.

Changes in metal oxidation states also likely occur during CDI. Antibiotic treatment and CDI in murine models increase tissue oxygenation in colonic epithelial cells [39,40]. Oxygen diffusing into the lumen can then oxidize soluble ferrous Fe (Fe^2+) to form less soluble Fe^3+ (Figure2). Changes in metal accessibility due to oxidation, along with sequestration by host immune proteins and the risk of heme toxicity during CDI, contribute to a dynamic intestinal environment in which *C. difficile* must adapt to continue thriving.

## Adaptation to nutrient metal availability### Iron regulationFe is limited in the inflamed intestines due to chelation by host Fe-binding proteins [41] and environmental conditions that oxidize Fe^2+ to Fe^3+, as discussed in the previous section (Figure2). In *C. difficile*, the relative abundance of Fe is detected primarily by the ferric-uptake regulator (Fur), which dimerizes under Fe-replete conditions and binds DNA [42]. As in other bacteria [42], Fur typically represses gene transcription when bound to Fe [43]. Fur de-repression in an Fe-deplete environment results in the transcription of multiple genes with roles in metallostasis and metabolism [44]. Amongst the genes involved in metallostasis are *zupT* (zinc transporter), a putative *fhu* operon (predicted siderophore uptake), and *feo* genes (ferrous iron importers) (Figure3) [44]. ZupT is highly expressed under Fe-limited and Zn-limited conditions [43,45–47] and imports Zn in response to CP [45]. The expression of a Zn-importer under Fe-starvation may be due to CP-mediated chelation of both metals under physiological conditions [28]. Thus, during infection the presence of CP may indicate both Zn and Fe limitation, prompting *C. difficile* to respond to reduced availability of these metals. Alternatively, *C. difficile* may have evolved cross-regulation of Fe and Zn starvation responses similar to those described in *Staphylococcus aureus* where both Fur and a Zn-uptake regulator (Zur) coordinate transcription of the metallophore staphylopine [48]. Though no functional Zur is described in *C. difficile*, other regulatory mechanisms may mediate cross-regulation with multiple stress responses. This type of non-Fur family regulation was observed in *Clostridium beijerinckii*, where Fe^2+ bound to AdhR de-repressed metabolic *adhA1* and *adhA2* expression [49].

Genes in the *fhu* operon are homologous to genes encoding siderophore uptake machinery [50]. However, no siderophore biosynthesis pathways are described for *C. difficile*, leaving the intriguing possibility that *C. difficile* may engage in siderophore piracy and transport microbiota-produced siderophores similar to *Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron* [51]. Ferrous Fe transporters encoded by *feoB* and *feoA* are widespread amongst Bacteria [52]. FeoB is an integrated membrane protein with a G-protein domain and provides the route of entry for Fe^2+ [52]. The function of cytosolic FeoA is unknown, though recent reports propose a model where FeoA interacts with FeoB to modulate GTPase activity and Fe import [53]. While most bacteria encode a single *feoAB* operon [52], *C. difficile* encodes multiple *feoAB* systems [40]. For instance, the common laboratory strain630 encodes3 *feoB* and5 *feoA* genes, with only some of these regulated by Fur [43,44]. Fe^2+ import via FeoAB can influence not only metallostasis, but can alter clinically relevant aspects of *C. difficile* physiology. Mutation of *feoB1* induces an Fe starvation response that shifts *C. difficile* metabolism towards pathways that decrease sensitivity to the antibiotic metronidazole [54], which served as a frontline antibiotic in the treatment of CDI [55]. The factors that regulate the production of *C. difficile* *feoA* and *feoB* and how multiple copies of these genes enable *C. difficile* to survive in the intestines warrant further investigation.

Fe limitation also alters transcription of metabolic genes (for a full view, see Ref. [44]). For example, proline reduction via a proline reductase supports energy production and the maintenance of redox balance. Increased availability of proline or hydroxyproline, a component of collagen, opens a niche from which *C. difficile* can compete with the microbiota [46,56]. Under Fe-limited conditions, *C. difficile* increases transcription of proline reductase genes, shifting energy production from glycolysis to amino acid fermentation via Stickland reactions [44]. Decreased flux through central carbon catabolic pathways, largely owing to reliance of those pathways on Fe-containing ferredoxins and Fe-sulfur clusters, is a key metabolic signature for *C. difficile* in a Fe-deplete environment.

### Heme stressThe influx of heme during CDI could potentiate detrimental reactive oxygen species generation [41]. To limit the toxic effects of heme overabundance, *C. difficile* encodes two systems that are specifically responsive to heme. Heme binding to the transcriptional regulator HatR will de-repress transcription of *hatT*, an efflux pump that limits intracellular heme concentrations [36]. Under high heme stress, the regulator HsmR activates transcription of *HsmA* to incorporate heme into the cytoplasmic membrane [57]. Heme-bound HsmA decreases sensitivity to oxidative stress and to the antibiotic vancomycin [57], a current first-line treatment for primary CDI [55]. Additionally, mutations in *hsmA* can confer resistance to metronidazole [58]. Combined, the HatRT and HsmRA systems protect *C. difficile* from toxic concentrations of heme released during inflammation.

### Zinc regulationIn response to CP-mediated Zn sequestration, *C. difficile* upregulates transcription of many of the same genes that increase under Fe limitation [44,46], similar to that observed in *Acinetobacter baumannii* where CP elicited a dual Zn/Fe response [28]. As part of the response to CP, *C. difficile* upregulates *zupT*, which enables *C. difficile* to overcome CP Zn sequestration in a mouse model of CDI [45]. The selenoprotein proline reductase is also more highly transcribed in *C. difficile* grown with CP [46]. Furthermore, deletion of selenoprotein synthesis inhibits function of the proline reductase with concomitant increases in the expression of genes involved in mannitol catabolism, possibly shifting *C. difficile* metabolism away from Stickland fermentation and towards mannitol fermentation [59]. Combined, these studies suggest an interplay between Zn and selenoprotein synthesis that influence metabolic pathways relevant to *C. difficile* colonization.

### Other nutrient metalsAside from Fe and Zn, there is limited data on the roles other nutrient metals play in *C. difficile* physiology and virulence. *C. difficile* encodes two Mn-dependent catalases (*cotD*, *cotG*) and a Mn-dependent superoxide dismutase (*sodA*) that are associated with the spore coat [60]. As these genes are not upregulated in the presence of oxygen [40,61], it is likely they perform a function associated with spore maturation or protection rather than ROS detoxification in vegetative *C. difficile*. Mn is also required for fibronectin-binding in Fbp68, which may enable *C. difficile* attachment to host cell surfaces during infection [62]. Recently, a putative Ni-uptake system was identified through comparison of a peptide transporter in *C. difficile*, OppA, with the Ni-binding protein NikZ in *Campylobacter jejuni* [63]. This led to the hypothesis that OppA functions as a Ni-transporter for Ni-dependent enzymes like urease [63]. However, the full contribution of Mn and Ni availability on CDI remain an open area of exploration.

## ConclusionFrom the initial changes to the microbiota that increase colonization susceptibility to the subsequent immune response, changes in nutrient metal accessibility influence *C. difficile* colonization and survival. While there are many unknown details in how *C. difficile* controls its responses to environmental fluctuations in metal concentrations, there are two areas in particular that may be fruitful topics for future exploration. First, how does *C. difficile* sense and respond to limitation of non-Fe metals like Zn and Mn and what are the effects on *C. difficile* colonization? Second, how does metal availability impact *C. difficile* interactions with the microbiota? Illuminating these avenues of the host-microbe-*C. difficile* interplay will ultimately broaden our understanding of the adaptation strategies inherent in this pathogen.
