# Inhibition of spores to prevent the recurrence of *Clostridioides difficile* infection - A possibility or an improbability?

Chun-Wei Chiu<sup>a</sup>, Pei-Jane Tsai<sup>b</sup>, Ching-Chi Lee<sup>c</sup>, Wen-Chien Ko<sup>c,d,\*</sup>, Yuan-Pin Hung<sup>a,c,\*\*</sup>

## Abstract*Clostridioides difficile* is one of the most common nosocomial gastrointestinal pathogens, and recurrence is a problematic issue because approximately20–30% of patients experience at least one episode of recurrence, even after treatment with a therapeutic drug of choice for *C. difficile* infection (CDI), such as vancomycin. CDI recurrence has a multifactorial complex mechanism, in which gut microbiota disruption coincident with viable *C. difficile* spores is considered the most important factor. The effectiveness of an anti-*C. difficile* antimicrobial agent against CDI cannot guarantee its inhibitory effect on *C. difficile* spores and vice versa. However, an antimicrobial agent, such as fidaxomicin, which has a good inhibitory effect on both *C. difficile* vegetative cells and spores is assumed to not only treat CDI but also prevent its recurrence. Prolonged adherence to the exosporium has been proposed as a possible mechanism of inhibiting spores, and as a result, redesigning anti-*C. difficile* antimicrobial agents with the ability to adhere to the exosporium may provide another pathway for the development of anti-*C. difficile* spore agents. For example, vancomycin lacks an inhibitory effect against *C. difficile* spores, but a vancomycin-loaded spore-targeting iron oxide nanoparticle that selectively binds to *C. difficile* spores has been developed to successfully delay spore germination. Some new antimicrobial agents in phase II clinical trials, including cadazolid and ridinilazole, have shown exceptional anti-*C. difficile* and spore-inhibiting effects that can be expected to not only treat CDI but also prevent its recurrence in the future.

## Introduction*Clostridioides difficile* is one of the most common nosocomial gastrointestinal pathogens, with clinical presentations ranging from mild diarrhea and pseudomembranous colitis to toxic megacolon, with a mortality of up to25–40% [1–9]. Severe colitis develops in approximately3% of patients with *C. difficile* infection (CDI) who may have paradoxical decrease in the amount of diarrhea after ileus or toxic megacolon developed [3,10]. Diagnosis of CDI relies on the detection of toxigenic *C. difficile*, its toxins or toxin genes in stool [9,11].

Metronidazole was suggested for mild CDI and vancomycin was recommended for severe CDI [8]. Nevertheless, oral vancomycin and fidaxomicin have been suggested to replace metronidazole as the therapeutic choice for either mild-moderate or severe CDI in the Clinical Practice Guidelines for *C. difficile* Infection in Adults and Children:2017 Update by the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) and Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America (SHEA) [12]. For patients with severe and fulminant forms of CDI presenting as ileus, oral intake of vancomycin or fidaxomicin is disrupted because of the obstruction of the gut because fidaxomicin can only be administered orally; and vancomycin, either administered orally or intra-rectally. This leads to marked disruption of the microbiota, leading to prolonged loss of colonization resistance to *C. difficile* and dense colonization by vancomycin-resistant *Enterococcus*, *K. pneumoniae*, and *E. coli* [13].

Although antimicrobial resistance is not clinically problematic, treating CDI with metronidazole and vancomycin is associated with a high treatment failure rate and high relapse rate [14]. According to the Clinical Practice Guidelines by IDSA/SHEA, oral vancomycin was still the drug of choice for recurrent *C. difficile* associated diarrhea, but the rate of second recurrence rate is as high as22.6–41.8%, despite successful treatment with vancomycin [15]. The high recurrence rate of CDI after treatment might be due to the generation of spores that lead to persistence of CDI [16,17].

## The biology of spores in the pathogenesis of *C. difficile*

Owing to its strict anaerobic requirements, the infectious and transmissible morphotype of *C. difficile* is a dormant spore, which is characterized by resistance to heat, oxygen, and common disinfectants, such as ethanol-based hand sanitizers [16,18,19]. The spore resistance properties are attributed to a low water content (25–60% of wet weight), elevated levels of dipicolinic acid (25% of core dry weight) chelated with calcium, and the saturation of DNA with α/β-type small acid soluble proteins [16,20].

The spores of *C. difficile* have the innermost spore compartment known as the spore core, which contains the spore DNA, RNA and most enzymes [16]. The spore core is encased in three protective layers: the first is the compressed inner membrane protein surrounded by the germ cell wall, which has a very low permeability to small molecules, including water, and protects the core from DNA-damaging molecules; the second is the peptidoglycan cortex; a coat that predominantly consists of proteins; and the third is the exosporium (the outermost layer), which predominantly consists of glycoproteins [16].

During CDI, *C. difficile* initiates a sporulation pathway that produces dormant spores that lead to persistence and dissemination of CDI within hospitalized patients, and the pathway is triggered by environmental stimuli such as nutrient starvation, quorum sensing and other unidentified stress factors [16,17]. When *C. difficile* spores are ingested, the spores are capable of enduring the acidic conditions of the stomach and then transition to a metabolically active state (germinate) in the small intestine [21]. Germination of *C. difficile* spores and the growth of vegetative forms take place only in the lower gastrointestinal tract where the oxygen concentration is very low [22,23]. Some contents of the intestine, such as bile acids, induce germination of the spores into an actively replicating vegetative cell [23,24]. Taurocholate, a conjugated primary bile acid in the small intestine, has the ability to promote germination and to influence vegetative growth of *C. difficile*; in contrast, chenodeoxycholate, another primary bile acid, inhibits spore germination in *C. difficile* and acts both as a competitive inhibitor of taurocholate and a suppressor of vegetative growth [24,25]. The efficiency of germination varies between clinical strains of *C. difficile* and whether it is associated with ribotype, disease severity or therapeutic failure needs further investigation [23,26].

## Rationale of the implication of spore inhibition for the prevention of recurrence of *C. difficile* infection: is it the absolute and only solution?

Recurrence is a problematic issue in CDI, with20–30% of patients facing at least one episode of recurrence even when treated with a therapeutic drug of choice for CDI [21,27]. In a simulated CDI gut model, although vancomycin successfully treated the initial episode of CDI, recurrence occurred approximately18 days after therapy cessation, in which the germination of spores of *C. difficile* in the environment of a biofilm was noted [28]. The recurrence rates of clinical patients with CDI receiving metronidazole and vancomycin therapy, two of the most commonly used anti-*C. difficile* antibiotics, were27.1% and24.0%, respectively [27]. The recurrence could be due to either reinfection of a susceptible individual or reactivation of latent spores within the individual's gastrointestinal tract [27].

CDI recurrence has a multi-factorial complex mechanism, but gut microbiota disruption coincident with viable *C. difficile* spores is considered as the most important factor [28,29]. Of course, targeting spores of *C. difficile* is not the only way to resolve the troublesome CDI recurrence problem; multiple factors, including gut microbiota status and the ribotype of the *C. difficile* (with different spore germination efficiency) also influence the recurrence rate of CDI [26]. For example, rifaximin, though lacking spore-inhibiting effects, can prevent the recurrence of CDI when given immediately after completing standard anti-CDI antibiotics through its relative sparing of enteric microbiota [30–32]. The ribotype of *C. difficile* with different spore germination efficiencies will influence the recurrent rate of CDI; for example, ribotype027 isolates exhibit significantly higher germination efficiencies in the presence of sodium taurocholate than ribotype106 and ribotype078 [26].

Even with the influence of the gut microbiota condition and the ribotype of CDI, developing antimicrobial agents with both anti-*C. difficile* and spore-inhibiting effects have been proven to provide a way to not only treat CDI but also prevent its recurrence, including fidaxomicin, one of the drugs of choice for CDI, and cadazolid and ridinilazole, two antimicrobial agents still in phase II clinical trials (Table1) [33–37].

## The application of the spore-inhibiting effect of current anti-*C. difficile* antimicrobial agents in the therapy of *C. difficile* infection and its recurrenceThe effectiveness of an anti-*C. difficile* antimicrobial agent toward CDI cannot guarantee its inhibitory effect on spores of *C. difficile* and vice versa [38–40]. Though many antimicrobial agents are regarded as drugs of choice for CDI, such as fidaxomicin and vancomycin, their inhibitory effects on *C. difficile* spores are diverse, which might be due to their different adherent and inhibitory effects toward spores of *C. difficile* [38,39]; in contrast, tigecycline and piperacillin/tazobactam, two commonly used antimicrobial agents in the clinic, both have a dose-dependent inhibitory effect on spore production at subminimum inhibitory concentrations (sub-MICs) in-vitro, but their clinical application is limited because of their inadequate therapeutic effect on CDI [16,40,41]. If an antimicrobial agent, such as fidaxomicin, has both anti-vegetative cell and anti-spore effects toward *C. difficile*, it is assumed to be able to not only achieve a therapeutic effect but also lower the recurrence rate [33]. In contrast, if an antimicrobial agent, such as vancomycin, has only anti-vegetative cell effects but lacks anti-spore effects toward *C. difficile*, it will be hypothetically only achieving a therapeutic effect but with a possible high recurrence rate after therapy unless there is some modification of the antimicrobial agents to strengthen its effect on spores [38,39].

Fidaxomicin, a drug used for CDI therapy, can inhibit both the sporulation and the outgrowth of a vegetative cell from the germinating spore, but its effect on the germination of *C. difficile* spores is limited (Table1) [30,42–45]. Fidaxomicin has a persistent inhibitory effect on sporulation when added to early-stationary-phase cells in *C. difficile* strains, including the epidemic NAP1/BI/027 strain at sub-MICs, and thus prevents subsequent growth and toxin production in-vitro; in contrast, vancomycin and metronidazole do not inhibit sporulation at similar sub-MICs [30,43,44]. In contrast to its inhibitory effect on sporulation, fidaxomicin was found to have no inhibitory effect on the initiation of *C. difficile* spore germination by analyzing the kinetics of the initiation of spore germination [42].

The spore-inhibiting effect of fidaxomicin has been illustrated in clinical trials, although the clinical cure rates with fidaxomicin were similar to those with vancomycin in both the modified intention-to-treat analysis (88.2% with fidaxomicin and85.8% with vancomycin) and per-protocol analysis (92.1% and89.8%, respectively), significantly fewer patients in the fidaxomicin group than in the vancomycin group had a recurrence of the infection, in both the modified intention-to-treat analysis (15.4% vs.25.3%, P=0.005) and per-protocol analysis (13.3% vs.24.0%, P=0.004) [46]. The lower recurrence rate following fidaxomicin therapy compared to vancomycin was also due to its lower effect on the microbiota [15,47–50]. In the mice model, fidaxomicin reduced the proportion of Clostridial growth to a lesser extent, but increased that of Bacteroidia, resulting in less disturbance in microbiota diversity [47]. The lesser influence on the microbiota composition of fidaxomicin, compared to vancomycin, promoted faster microbial recovery in the gut, and had more colonization resistance to *C. difficile* [48–50].

Prolonged adherence to the exosporium has been proposed as a possible way of inhibiting spores; as a result, redesigning anti-*C. difficile* antimicrobial agents with the ability to adhere to the exosporium may provide another pathway for the development of anti-*C. difficile* spore agents [38,39]. Vancomycin, although considered as one of the drugs of choice for CDI, was found to have no inhibitory effect on the initiation of *C. difficile* spore germination by analyzing the kinetics of the initiation of spore germination [42]. Thus, the recurrence rate after vancomycin therapy was as high as24.0% [27]. Although vancomycin itself has no effect on *C. difficile* spores, a vancomycin-loaded spore-targeting iron oxide nanoparticle (van-IONP) that selectively binds to *C. difficile* spores has been developed to successfully delay spore germination [38].

## Potential future armament for inhibiting *C. difficile* spores and preventing recurrence of *C. difficile* infectionSome antimicrobial agents undergoing clinical trials have shown excellent anti-*C. difficile* and spore-inhibiting effects, which can not only treat CDI but also prevent its recurrence (Table1) [33–37].

Cadazolid, a novel fluoroquinolone-oxazolidinone antibiotic in phase II and III trials currently, exhibits potent in-vitro activity against *C. difficile* and its spores [33,34], and has limited effect on the gut microflora, including the *B. fragilis* group and *Lactobacillus* spp. [51,52]. The cure rates of patients with CDI that received250,500, or1000 mg cadazolid twice daily were76.5%,80.0%,68.4%, respectively, compared to68.2% in the group treated with125 mg vancomycin four times daily; in addition, each dosage of cadazolid resulted in a lower recurrence rate than vancomycin (18.2–25.0% vs.50%, respectively) [33]. It is a pity in phase III study cadazolid did not achieve its primary endpoint of non-inferiority to vancomycin for clinical cure in one of two phase III *C. difficile* infection trials [51]. Nevertheless pooling the data from two studies with three randomized controlled trials, cadazolid revealed no significantly difference in sustained clinical response at clinical follow-up (pooled odds ratio (OR)=1.14; P=0.27) but lower recurrence rate (pooled OR=0.71; P=0.04) compared to vancomycin [53].

Ridinilazole, a novel class of heterocyclic antibacterial drugs, can inhibit DNA bacterial synthesis and thus inhibit the growth of *C. difficile* [36,53]. Treatment with supra-MIC concentrations (4× and40× MIC) of ridinilazole resulted in a significant reduction in vegetative cells over72 h (4 log difference, P<0.01) compared with controls without inducing spore formation [36,54]. Ridinilazole, compared to vancomycin, had almost no effect on the gut microbiota, including several Bacteroidaceae and Clostridiaceae families, and maintained the intestinal bile acid profile [35,55]. In phase II trials, the sustained clinical response in patients in the ridinilazole group was66.7%, which was superior to that of42.4% in the vancomycin group [35]. Of note, the recurrence rate of CDI among patients receiving ridinilazole was14.3%, which was lower than that of34.8% for vancomycin [35].

Ramoplanin, a glycolipodepsipeptide antibiotic that is active against *C. difficile*, results in spore counts consistently below the level of detection for28 days after even a brief (30-min) exposure in-vitro [39]. In the same study investigating exposure to ramoplanin, an increase in spore counts was noted after removal of the *C. difficile* exosporium, which suggests that rather than being directly sporicidal, ramoplanin adheres to the exosporium for a prolonged period, and during this time it is available to attack the germinating cells [39]. In hamster models of CDI, *C. difficile* spores were recovered significantly more often from the cecal contents of vancomycin-treated hamsters (n=19/23) compared with ramoplanin-treated (n=6/23) hamsters (P<0.05) [37]. It is unfortunate that though ramoplanin revealed inhibitory effects on *C. difficile* and its spores in-vitro, its clinical efficacy toward CDI and its recurrence is still lacking [32].

CRS3123, a fully synthetic diaryldiamine antimicrobial drug, prevents both *C. difficile* growth and spore production by inhibiting bacterial protein synthesis, with a >10-fold reduction in the sporulation rate in-vitro [56]. To date, only data from a phase I study assessing the safety and tolerability of CRS3123 are available, and evidence of its clinical effect on CDI and its recurrence is deficient [32].

Nisin, a bacteriocin produced by several strains of *Lactococcus lactis*, was noted to have inhibitory activity against clinical isolates of *C. difficile*, with spore viability reduced by40–50% in-vitro [57]. However, in-vivo evidence of nisin in treating CDI or its recurrence is absent.

Development of a vaccine targeting the spores of *C. difficile* provides a feasible pathway for preventing CDI recurrence [58]. Anti-spore IgY from chickens delayed the occurrence of diarrhea by1.5 d and the spore adherence to the colonic mucosa by90% in a mouse model, and co-administration of anti-spore IgY coupled with vancomycin delayed the appearance of recurrent diarrhea by a median of2 days [58].

Some small molecules and compounds were noted to have spore-inhibiting effects in-vitro, but a determination of their "real" clinical efficacy needs further clinical trials [59,60]. Carvacrol, a food-grade, plant-derived compound, significantly reduces sporulation, germination and spore outgrowth of *C. difficile* and downregulates critical genes involved in spore production in-vitro [59]. Rhodomyrtone, a bioactive compound derived from the leaves of the rose myrtle (*Rhodomyrtus tomentosa*), is four-fold more potent than vancomycin in inhibiting *C. difficile* spore outgrowth in-vitro [60].

## ConclusionDeveloping an antimicrobial agent with a good inhibitory effect on both *C. difficile* vegetative cells and spores can be expected to not only treat CDI but also prevent its recurrence, such as fidaxomicin. Cadazolid and ridinilazole possess a spore-inhibiting effect in-vitro, and thus decrease the recurrence of CDI after cessation of the drug in phase II and III clinical trials. Some small molecules and compounds were noted to have spore-inhibiting effects in-vitro, but the determination of their "real" clinical efficacy requires further clinical trials.
